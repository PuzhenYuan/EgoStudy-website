Abstract

Egocentric human manipulation data offer a scalable resource for training dexterous robot policies. However, how effectively robots can learn and benefit from these data remains unclear due to the embodiment gap between humans and robots. We present EgoStudy, a systematic, controlled study of egocentric data for robot learning. Holding the robot dataset fixed, we vary the scale and diversity of egocentric data, data processing strategies, and co-training configurations.

We investigate three questions: (1) Which data processing and pretraining choices most effectively exploit egocentric data? (2) Which components of robot action prediction benefit most from scaling these data? (3) How do scaling effects differ between vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs)?

Our experiments yield three main findings. First, data diversity, alignment between trajectories and detailed language annotations are key factors in effective transfer. Second, scaling egocentric data yields larger gains in predicting end-effector translation than in predicting fine-grained finger movements. Third, WAMs perform better when egocentric data are limited, whereas VLAs exhibit greater gains as the amount of egocentric data increases. Together, these findings provide practical guidance on processing and using egocentric data to train VLAs and WAMs, while clarifying the benefits and limitations of scaling these data.

Study Design

How can we isolate what a robot learns from human demonstrations? We vary the human data while keeping the robot training pool, expected robot exposure, and optimization budget fixed.

A shared action space

The VLA pairs Qwen3-VL-4B with a GR00T-style action expert. The WAM pairs a Wan2.1 14B video backbone with an action diffusion transformer. Both predict 32-step chunks in the same 108-dimensional action space. Each hand contributes a wrist pose and five fingertip poses.

A controlled data mixture

The human pool is curated from about 18,000 hours of raw video. We train with 1%, 3%, 10%, 30%, and 100% of each human source. At every scale, 75% of samples come from human demonstrations, 20% from Bimanual XHand, and 5% from RoboCasa-GR1.

Evaluation

On held-out robot episodes, we report masked mean squared error separately for wrist translation, wrist rotation, and fingertip pose, averaged over three training seeds. In RoboCasa-GR1, we also measure closed-loop task success after matched robot posttraining.

Does scaling egocentric data improve robot learning?

For each model, we expand the eligible human training pool from 1% to 100% and measure wrist translation error on held-out robot episodes.

Conclusion

On Bimanual XHand, wrist translation error falls by 20.2% for the VLA and 18.8% for the WAM. The corresponding reductions on RoboCasa-GR1 are 14.6% and 9.6%. The VLA gains more with scale, while the WAM generally reaches lower error.

Do pretraining gains carry over to robot control?

We posttrain each policy on the official RoboCasa-GR1 dataset under the same protocol and run ten trials per task, for 240 trials per evaluation.

Conclusion

From 1% to 100% egocentric data, RoboCasa-GR1 success rises from 38.3% to 43.8% for the VLA and from 39.6% to 45.4% for the WAM. At all three evaluated scales, lower validation error accompanies higher rollout success.

Which data processing and co-training choices improve transfer?

Four comparisons examine how clips are segmented, what their captions describe, which sources enter the mixture, and how action targets are spaced in time.

Conclusion

Clips that contain a complete action and captions that describe it in detail reduce wrist error on both holdouts. The mixed-source condition also outperforms the EgoDex comparison, indicating data diversity matters in effective pretraining. Fixed-step sampling works better for the VLA, and event-level sampling works better for the WAM.

Which robot motions benefit most from more human data?

Separating the motion of the wrist from the pose of each fingertip reveals where the aggregate change in prediction error comes from.

Conclusion

Wrist translation shows the clearest gain. Wrist rotation and fingertip pose show no consistent improvement across models and domains. The authors suggest that tracking errors and differences in hand–object contact may limit transfer for fine movements.

Do human and robot visual features become more alike?

Relative separation measures the distance between the mean human and robot features, normalized by their spread within each domain. A smaller value indicates closer feature distributions.

Conclusion

Human and robot features draw closer in the final VLA layer on both robot datasets. Modeling actions alone encourages the VLA to learn more similar features from human and robot observations. The four WAM layers show no consistent decrease. One possible explanation is that predicting future frames rewards the WAM for retaining differences in how human and robot hands look.

How does backbone choice affect scaling gains?

We compare two Qwen3-VL backbones for the VLA and two Wan video backbones for the WAM, tracking wrist translation error as human data increases.

Conclusion

The 4B and 8B Qwen3-VL backbones gain similarly from more human data. The Wan2.1 14B WAM gains more than the Wan2.2 5B variant. A small VLA backbone can learn effectively from egocentric video, while human-to-robot transfer in the WAM depends more heavily on a strong video backbone.

Results

EgoStudy brings egocentric human and bimanual robot demonstrations into a shared dexterous action space for controlled VLA and WAM pretraining and evaluation, enabling effective human-to-robot transfer. We conduct comprehensive experiments of data scale, semantic completeness, caption detail, source diversity, temporal sampling, action components and backbone capability, identifying how these choices affect robot foundation model pretraining.

First, data diversity, alignment between trajectories and detailed language annotations are key factors in effective transfer. Second, scaling egocentric data yields larger gains in predicting end-effector translation than in predicting fine-grained finger movements. Third, WAMs perform better when egocentric data are limited, whereas VLAs exhibit greater gains as the amount of egocentric data increases.

Experiments involving visual features and backbone variations indicate that the scaling of VLA may stem from the modeling of shared actions, whereas the scaling of WAM may depend on the video backbone’s capacity to assimilate egocentric video data—with actions merely being extracted from the video prediction. Together, these findings reveal the capability gains and limitations of scaling egocentric data.
